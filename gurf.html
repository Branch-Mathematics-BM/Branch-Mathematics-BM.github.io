GURF

A mathematical framework for determining which distinctions in a system are necessary for a task, whether those distinctions survive observation, compression, evolution, constraints, and intervention, and whether lawful response or continuation remains possible.

1. What GURF Is

GURF is a mathematical framework for lawful system responses.

Its central concern is not merely the state of a system, but whether the information currently available about that state is sufficient for a specified task, continuation, intervention, or response.

The governing question can be written conceptually as:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What must remain distinguishable now for the required response to remain possible later?} } \]

GURF therefore studies systems together with their observations, constraints, regimes, interventions, tasks, and lawful responses.

2. Relationship to Branch Mathematics

Branch Mathematics is the broader meta-structural mathematics governing lawful representation, transformation, composition, interfaces, realization, export, and re-entry.

GURF specializes that broader machinery to problems that possess genuine response semantics.

Schematically:

\[ \boxed{ \text{BM} \supset \text{response-specialized BM structure} = \text{GURF} } \]

BM does not need to be interpreted as a response theory.

GURF becomes relevant when the mathematics specifically asks what a system can observe, preserve, lose, recover, continue, or produce in response to a task or intervention.

3. Basic System Object

Let

\[ X \]

denote the underlying state space of a system.

A state is

\[ x\in X. \]

The space \(X\) may be finite, continuous, discrete, geometric, stochastic, functional, infinite-dimensional, or otherwise structured.

GURF does not assume one universal type of state space.

4. Tasks

A task specifies what information must ultimately be determined or what response must be produced.

A deterministic task may be represented as

\[ T: X \longrightarrow W, \]

where \(W\) is the task-output space.

Examples include:

prediction · classification · control · reconstruction · decision · continuation · estimation · admissibility testing · physical response

GURF is therefore task-relative.

Information that is irrelevant to one task may be essential to another.

5. Observations

Suppose the full state \(x\in X\) is not directly available.

Instead, the system is observed through a map

\[ r: X \longrightarrow Y. \]

The observed state is

\[ y = r(x). \]

The map \(r\) may represent measurement, compression, coarse-graining, projection, filtering, sensor output, discretization, or another information-reducing operation.

6. Observational Equivalence

Observation induces an equivalence relation:

\[ x \sim_r x' \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r(x) = r(x'). \]

Thus two underlying states become indistinguishable whenever the observation map gives them the same observed value.

The observational class of \(x\) is

\[ [x]_r = \left\{ x'\in X: r(x')=r(x) \right\}. \]

7. Observation Fibres

The fibre over an observed value \(y\in Y\) is

\[ r^{-1}(y) = \left\{ x\in X: r(x)=y \right\}. \]

The fibre represents every underlying state still compatible with the observation.

If

\[ |r^{-1}(y)|>1, \]

then the observation does not uniquely identify the underlying state.

8. Multiple Observations

Suppose observations are indexed by

\[ \alpha\in A \]

with maps

\[ r_\alpha: X \longrightarrow Y_\alpha. \]

The combined observation is

\[ r_A(x) = \left( r_\alpha(x) \right)_{\alpha\in A}. \]

If the observed values are

\[ \ell = \left( \ell_\alpha \right)_{\alpha\in A}, \]

then the states compatible with all observations form the completion fibre.

9. Completion Fibre

\[ \boxed{ \mathfrak C_A(\ell) = \bigcap_{\alpha\in A} r_\alpha^{-1}(\ell_\alpha) } \]

The completion fibre is the set of all underlying states satisfying every observation in the active observation family.

If

\[ \mathfrak C_A(\ell) = \varnothing, \]

then the observation set is mutually inconsistent.

If

\[ \left| \mathfrak C_A(\ell) \right| = 1, \]

then the observations identify a unique compatible state.

But unique state reconstruction is not always required.

A task may be recoverable even when many underlying states remain possible.

10. Exact Task Sufficiency

Let

\[ T: X \longrightarrow W \]

be the task of interest.

The observation \(r\) is sufficient for \(T\) when there exists

\[ \widetilde T: Y \longrightarrow W \]

such that

\[ \boxed{ T = \widetilde T \circ r }. \]

This means the task can be computed entirely from the observed representation.

11. Fibre Criterion for Exact Sufficiency

Exact sufficiency is equivalent to requiring that the task be constant on every observation fibre:

\[ r(x) = r(x') \] \[ \Longrightarrow \] \[ T(x) = T(x'). \]

Therefore information may be discarded safely whenever the discarded distinctions do not alter the required task.

Conversely, if

\[ r(x)=r(x') \quad\text{but}\quad T(x)\neq T(x'), \]

then the observation has destroyed information necessary for the task.

12. Completion-Fibre Task Criterion

Using the completion fibre, exact task determination requires

\[ T(x) = T(x') \qquad \forall x,x' \in \mathfrak C_A(\ell). \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ \left| T\left( \mathfrak C_A(\ell) \right) \right| = 1 }. \]

The completion fibre may therefore contain many states while still being perfectly sufficient for the task.

13. Approximate Sufficiency

Exact equality may be too strong in numerical, statistical, experimental, or physical settings.

Let \(d_W\) be a metric on task space \(W\).

A representation may be approximately sufficient at tolerance \(\varepsilon\) when

\[ d_W \left( T(x), T(x') \right) \le \varepsilon \]

whenever

\[ r(x)=r(x'). \]

Equivalently, one may require a reconstruction

\[ \widetilde T: Y \longrightarrow W \]

satisfying

\[ d_W \left( T(x), \widetilde T(r(x)) \right) \le \varepsilon. \]

The metric and tolerance must be stated explicitly.

14. Response Relations

A response need not always be represented by an ordinary function.

A general response relation may be written

\[ \mathcal R: X \rightrightarrows Y. \]

For a state \(x\), the admissible responses are

\[ \mathcal R(x) \subseteq Y. \]

A deterministic response is the special case

\[ \left| \mathcal R(x) \right| = 1 \]

for every admissible \(x\).

15. Continuation

Continuation asks whether the current representation contains enough information to support a required future response.

Suppose evolution is described by

\[ \Phi_t: X \longrightarrow X. \]

A future task at time \(t\) is

\[ T_t = T \circ \Phi_t. \]

Observation \(r\) remains sufficient for that future task if there exists

\[ \widetilde T_t: Y \longrightarrow W \]

satisfying

\[ \boxed{ T \circ \Phi_t = \widetilde T_t \circ r }. \]

16. Continuation Failure

Suppose two states are currently observationally indistinguishable:

\[ r(x) = r(x'). \]

If evolution produces

\[ T(\Phi_t(x)) \neq T(\Phi_t(x')), \]

then the current representation is insufficient for the future task.

The missing distinction may have been irrelevant at the observation time but becomes relevant after evolution.

17. Regimes

System laws may depend on an active regime.

Let

\[ \lambda \in \Lambda \]

denote a regime parameter.

Evolution may then take the form

\[ \Phi_t^{(\lambda)}: X \longrightarrow X. \]

Different regimes may therefore induce different continuation conditions, admissible responses, or required distinctions.

Regime information is part of the law context and should not be silently omitted when it changes the system response.

18. Law Passports

A response problem may require not only state information but information identifying the law or regime governing the state.

Schematically, one may treat the effective response context as

\[ (x,\lambda) \in X\times\Lambda. \]

A law Passport records the law-context distinctions necessary for lawful interpretation or continuation.

Thus two identical state descriptions may still require different responses if they belong to different active regimes.

19. Constraints

Let the admissible state set be

\[ K \subseteq X. \]

A lawful response may require

\[ x\in K. \]

Under evolution, viability may require

\[ \Phi_t(x) \in K \qquad \forall t \text{ in the required interval}. \]

Constraints therefore affect both the available state space and the admissible continuation.

20. Interventions

Let

\[ u \in U \]

denote an intervention or control.

A controlled evolution may be represented by

\[ \Phi_t: X\times U \longrightarrow X. \]

or more generally by

\[ \Phi_t^{(\lambda,u)} \]

when both regime and intervention matter.

A representation that is sufficient without intervention may become insufficient once interventions alter which distinctions matter.

21. Response Under Intervention

Suppose a task depends on both state and intervention:

\[ T: X\times U \longrightarrow W. \]

If only \(r(x)\) is retained, exact intervention-aware sufficiency requires a map

\[ \widetilde T: Y\times U \longrightarrow W \]

such that

\[ \boxed{ T(x,u) = \widetilde T(r(x),u) }. \]

22. Obstruction

An obstruction occurs when the currently retained information does not determine the response required by the task.

For an observation value \(y\), define the task image of its fibre:

\[ T(r^{-1}(y)) = \left\{ T(x): x\in r^{-1}(y) \right\}. \]

Exact recovery fails whenever

\[ \left| T(r^{-1}(y)) \right| > 1. \]

The spread of possible task values inside one observational fibre is a direct mathematical signature of task obstruction.

23. Repair

Repair introduces additional information sufficient to eliminate an identified obstruction.

Suppose an additional observation is

\[ q: X \longrightarrow Z. \]

The repaired observation becomes

\[ (r,q): X \longrightarrow Y\times Z. \]

Repair succeeds for task \(T\) when

\[ (r,q)(x) = (r,q)(x') \] \[ \Longrightarrow \] \[ T(x) = T(x'). \]

Thus repair does not require reconstructing every lost detail.

It requires restoring enough information for the task.

24. Minimal Repair Question

A natural optimization problem is to seek the smallest additional information that restores task sufficiency.

Schematically:

\[ \boxed{ \min_q \operatorname{Cost}(q) } \] \[ \text{subject to} \] \[ T = \widetilde T \circ (r,q). \]

The cost may represent dimension, bandwidth, number of measurements, computational complexity, energy, storage, experimental effort, or another domain-specific quantity.

25. Serial Information Loss

Suppose information passes through

\[ X \xrightarrow{r} Y \xrightarrow{s} Z. \]

If

\[ r(x) = r(x'), \]

then necessarily

\[ s(r(x)) = s(r(x')). \]

A deterministic downstream transformation cannot distinguish states that an upstream deterministic transformation has already merged.

26. Parallel Information Gain

If two observations are available,

\[ r: X\longrightarrow Y, \] \[ q: X\longrightarrow Z, \]

then the joint observation is

\[ (r,q): X \longrightarrow Y\times Z. \]

Its fibre satisfies

\[ (r,q)^{-1}(y,z) = r^{-1}(y) \cap q^{-1}(z). \]

Joint observation can therefore refine the candidate state set and remove an obstruction that neither observation removes individually.

27. Joint Feasibility

Suppose task \(c\) admits a feasible set

\[ P_c \subseteq X. \]

Solving every task separately does not automatically establish that one common state solves them all.

Joint feasibility requires

\[ \boxed{ \bigcap_{c\in\mathcal C} P_c \neq \varnothing }. \]

This is the difference between separate witnesses and one shared witness.

28. Coupling and Witness Reuse

In many systems, separate marginal possibilities do not determine a valid joint realization.

If two outputs have admissible marginals

\[ \mu \quad\text{and}\quad \nu, \]

a joint law requires a coupling

\[ \gamma \in \Gamma(\mu,\nu). \]

GURF therefore requires explicit attention to whether different tasks are allowed to use different witnesses or require one compatible joint realization.

29. Stochastic Response

A stochastic system may be represented by a Markov kernel

\[ M: X \longrightarrow \mathcal P(Y), \]

where \(\mathcal P(Y)\) is a space of probability measures on \(Y\).

For a test function

\[ \varphi: Y \longrightarrow \mathbb R, \]

the induced expectation operator is

\[ \boxed{ M^\dagger\varphi(x) = \mathbb E[ \varphi(Y) \mid X=x ] }. \]

Stochastic response semantics must remain probabilistic.

They cannot be silently replaced by deterministic equivalence unless the required mathematical conditions are proved.

30. Stochastic Task Sufficiency

Suppose a task depends only on the response distribution produced by \(M\).

Observation \(r\) is sufficient for that stochastic response when there exists a kernel

\[ \widetilde M: Y \longrightarrow \mathcal P(Z) \]

such that

\[ \boxed{ M = \widetilde M \circ r }. \]

Informally, states merged by \(r\) must induce the same task-relevant response law.

31. Nondeterministic Response

A nondeterministic response relation may be written

\[ R \subseteq X\times Y. \]

For a target set

\[ U \subseteq Y, \]

existential reachability is

\[ \Diamond_R(U) = \left\{ x: \exists y\in U \text{ with } xRy \right\}. \]

Universal safety is

\[ \Box_R(U) = \left\{ x: \forall y,\, xRy \Rightarrow y\in U \right\}. \]

These are mathematically different requirements.

GURF therefore requires explicit existential, universal, path, witness, or strategy quantifiers whenever nondeterministic semantics are present.

32. Operation Order

Operations that look individually reasonable may fail to commute.

If

\[ F:X\longrightarrow Y \]

and

\[ G:X\longrightarrow Z, \]

then performing transformations in different orders may produce mathematically different results:

\[ F\circ G \neq G\circ F \]

whenever both compositions are meaningful but noncommuting.

In applied work this becomes important for operations such as filtering, differentiation, localization, reconstruction, coarse-graining, projection, intervention, and evolution.

33. Information Before and After an Operation

Suppose

\[ F: X \longrightarrow Y. \]

An operation changes the available distinction structure from that of \(X\) to that induced by \(F\).

A later task

\[ T: X \longrightarrow W \]

survives the operation only when

\[ F(x) = F(x') \] \[ \Longrightarrow \] \[ T(x) = T(x'). \]

This is the central information-preservation test repeated throughout GURF.

34. Response Equivalence

Two states may be considered equivalent relative to a task when they produce the same required response.

Define

\[ x \sim_T x' \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T(x) = T(x'). \]

Observation \(r\) is sufficient for \(T\) exactly when

\[ \sim_r \;\subseteq\; \sim_T. \]

In words: whenever the observation identifies two states, the task must also identify them.

35. Refinement Ordering

Suppose observations \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) induce equivalence relations

\[ \sim_{r_1} \quad\text{and}\quad \sim_{r_2}. \]

If

\[ \sim_{r_1} \subseteq \sim_{r_2}, \]

then \(r_1\) preserves at least as many distinctions as \(r_2\).

This gives a natural information-refinement ordering between representations.

36. Exact Reconstruction Is Stronger Than Task Sufficiency

Full state reconstruction requires the observation to distinguish every task-relevant state uniquely.

Exact reconstruction may require

\[ r(x)=r(x') \Longrightarrow x=x'. \]

Task sufficiency requires only

\[ r(x)=r(x') \Longrightarrow T(x)=T(x'). \]

Therefore:

\[ \boxed{ \text{state reconstruction} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{task sufficiency} } \]

but the converse need not hold.

37. GURF and Compression

Let

\[ C: X \longrightarrow Z \]

be a compression.

Compression is acceptable for task \(T\) when

\[ \exists \widetilde T: Z\longrightarrow W \]

such that

\[ T = \widetilde T \circ C. \]

Thus GURF does not ask whether compression loses information in the absolute sense.

It asks whether compression loses information required by the task.

38. GURF and Model Reduction

Suppose a high-dimensional state

\[ x\in X \]

is reduced to

\[ z = R(x) \in Z. \]

A reduced evolution

\[ \widetilde\Phi_t: Z \longrightarrow Z \]

is dynamically compatible when

\[ \boxed{ R\circ\Phi_t = \widetilde\Phi_t \circ R }. \]

Exact commutation may be relaxed only when the approximation criterion is stated explicitly.

39. Dynamic Closure

A reduced representation is dynamically closed when its future evolution can be determined from the reduced state itself.

If

\[ z = R(x), \]

dynamic closure requires an evolution law

\[ \widetilde\Phi_t(z) \]

independent of which underlying state in

\[ R^{-1}(z) \]

produced the reduced state.

If different hidden states with the same reduced state evolve into different task-relevant futures, closure fails.

40. Obstruction as Fibre Spread

For numerical task space \(W\), the ambiguity inside a fibre may be quantified.

For a metric \(d_W\), define the task diameter

\[ \operatorname{diam}_T \left( r^{-1}(y) \right) = \sup_{ x,x' \in r^{-1}(y) } d_W \left( T(x), T(x') \right). \]

Exact sufficiency corresponds to

\[ \operatorname{diam}_T \left( r^{-1}(y) \right) = 0. \]

Approximate sufficiency may require

\[ \operatorname{diam}_T \left( r^{-1}(y) \right) \le \varepsilon. \]

Quantities such as this are natural candidates for future MATLAB-style visualizations because they can be plotted across state, parameter, or regime space.

41. Response Surfaces

When a response is numerical and depends on parameters, GURF can study a response surface

\[ R: \Theta \longrightarrow \mathbb R^m, \]

where

\[ \theta \in \Theta \]

contains parameters, interventions, initial conditions, or regime values.

Scientific figures may then show:

response magnitude · sensitivity · stability boundaries · bifurcations · uncertainty · fibre diameter · repair cost · regime transitions · continuation failure

42. Sensitivity

When a differentiable response

\[ R: \mathbb R^n \longrightarrow \mathbb R^m \]

is available, local sensitivity is encoded by the Jacobian

\[ J_R(x) = \frac{\partial R}{\partial x}. \]

Directions in state space with large response derivatives may correspond to distinctions that become especially important for prediction or control.

Directions lying approximately in the kernel

\[ \ker J_R(x) \]

may locally have little first-order effect on that particular response.

43. Local Versus Global Sufficiency

Local insensitivity does not automatically imply global equivalence.

Even when

\[ J_R(x)v = 0, \]

finite displacement along \(v\) may still alter the response through higher-order terms or nonlinear structure.

Therefore local differential analysis and global fibre analysis must not be confused.

44. Why Nonlinearity Matters

Suppose

\[ R(x) \]

is nonlinear.

Then

\[ R \left( \lambda x + (1-\lambda)y \right) \]

need not equal

\[ \lambda R(x) + (1-\lambda)R(y). \]

Hence averaging, filtering, coarse-graining, or probabilistic mixing may alter response structure in ways that are invisible to linear analysis.

45. GURF as a Response Audit

A practical GURF analysis asks:

1. State: What is the underlying mathematical state?

2. Task: What response must ultimately be determined?

3. Observation: What information is actually available?

4. Fibre: Which underlying states remain compatible with that information?

5. Sufficiency: Is the task constant across the fibre?

6. Evolution: Does sufficiency survive future dynamics?

7. Regime: Which law context is active?

8. Intervention: Can external action change which distinctions matter?

9. Obstruction: Which unresolved distinctions block the response?

10. Repair: What additional information removes that obstruction?

46. What GURF Does Not Do

GURF does not make an invalid physical or mathematical model valid.

It does not replace probability theory, control theory, dynamical systems, PDE theory, information theory, geometry, statistics, or other established mathematics.

It does not establish physical realizability merely because a response relation can be written formally.

The mathematical structures used in a GURF construction remain governed by their native mathematics.

47. Research Semantics Must Stay Distinct

GURF distinguishes several mathematical situations that must not be silently merged:

Semantic type Representative structure
Deterministic \(F:X\to Y\)
Stochastic \(M:X\to\mathcal P(Y)\)
Nondeterministic \(R\subseteq X\times Y\)
Approximate metric, divergence, tolerance, or error bound
Controlled \(\Phi:X\times U\to X\)
Regime-dependent \(\Phi^{(\lambda)}\)

Determined by direct derivation and the mathematics of the structures involved.

External novelty

Must be evaluated separately from mathematical validity.

Not established merely by internal development, examples, or numerical success.

49. Scientific Visualization Policy

GURF research pages will include quantitative scientific figures whenever the equations produce meaningful numerical or geometric structure.

Appropriate figures include:

response curves · state-space plots · phase portraits · parameter sweeps · error surfaces · fibre-diameter plots · stability boundaries · heatmaps · spectra · vector fields · sensitivity maps · convergence plots · uncertainty bands

These figures will be used to expose mathematical behavior, not as decoration.

Structural GURF and BM diagrams will be reconstructed separately before being treated as canonical public figures.

50. Central GURF Principle

The central mathematical idea of GURF can be summarized as follows:

\[ \boxed{ \text{A representation is adequate only relative to the responses it must still support.} } \]

Losing information is therefore not automatically a failure.

The failure occurs when a distinction required by the current or future task is removed without a lawful way to reconstruct, replace, or repair it.
