GURF

A mathematical framework for determining which distinctions in a system are necessary for a task, whether those distinctions survive observation, compression, evolution, constraints, and intervention, and whether lawful response or continuation remains possible.

1. What GURF Is

GURF is a mathematical framework for lawful system responses.

Its central concern is not merely the state of a system, but whether the information currently available about that state is sufficient for a specified task, continuation, intervention, or response.

The governing question can be written conceptually as:

\[ \boxed{ \text{What must remain distinguishable now for the required response to remain possible later?} } \]

GURF therefore studies systems together with their observations, constraints, regimes, interventions, tasks, and lawful responses.

2. Relationship to Branch Mathematics

Branch Mathematics is the broader meta-structural mathematics governing lawful representation, transformation, composition, interfaces, realization, export, and re-entry.

GURF specializes that broader machinery to problems that possess genuine response semantics.

Schematically:

\[ \boxed{ \text{BM} \supset \text{response-specialized BM structure} = \text{GURF} } \]

BM does not need to be interpreted as a response theory.

GURF becomes relevant when the mathematics specifically asks what a system can observe, preserve, lose, recover, continue, or produce in response to a task or intervention.

3. Basic System Object

Let

\[ X \]

denote the underlying state space of a system.

A state is

\[ x\in X. \]

The space \(X\) may be finite, continuous, discrete, geometric, stochastic, functional, infinite-dimensional, or otherwise structured.

GURF does not assume one universal type of state space.

4. Tasks

A task specifies what information must ultimately be determined or what response must be produced.

A deterministic task may be represented as

\[ T: X \longrightarrow W, \]

where \(W\) is the task-output space.

Examples include:

prediction · classification · control · reconstruction · decision · continuation · estimation · admissibility testing · physical response

GURF is therefore task-relative.

Information that is irrelevant to one task may be essential to another.

5. Observations

Suppose the full state \(x\in X\) is not directly available.

Instead, the system is observed through a map

\[ r: X \longrightarrow Y. \]

The observed state is

\[ y = r(x). \]

The map \(r\) may represent measurement, compression, coarse-graining, projection, filtering, sensor output, discretization, or another information-reducing operation.

6. Observational Equivalence

Observation induces an equivalence relation:

\[ x \sim_r x' \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r(x) = r(x'). \]

Thus two underlying states become indistinguishable whenever the observation map gives them the same observed value.

The observational class of \(x\) is

\[ [x]_r = \left\{ x'\in X: r(x')=r(x) \right\}. \]

7. Observation Fibres

The fibre over an observed value \(y\in Y\) is

\[ r^{-1}(y) = \left\{ x\in X: r(x)=y \right\}. \]

The fibre represents every underlying state still compatible with the observation.

If

\[ |r^{-1}(y)|>1, \]

then the observation does not uniquely identify the underlying state.

8. Multiple Observations

Suppose observations are indexed by

\[ \alpha\in A \]

with maps

\[ r_\alpha: X \longrightarrow Y_\alpha. \]

The combined observation is

\[ r_A(x) = \left( r_\alpha(x) \right)_{\alpha\in A}. \]

If the observed values are

\[ \ell = \left( \ell_\alpha \right)_{\alpha\in A}, \]

then the states compatible with all observations form the completion fibre.

9. Completion Fibre

\[ \boxed{ \mathfrak C_A(\ell) = \bigcap_{\alpha\in A} r_\alpha^{-1}(\ell_\alpha) } \]

The completion fibre is the set of all underlying states satisfying every observation in the active observation family.

If

\[ \mathfrak C_A(\ell) = \varnothing, \]

then the observation set is mutually inconsistent.

If

\[ \left| \mathfrak C_A(\ell) \right| = 1, \]

then the observations identify a unique compatible state.

But unique state reconstruction is not always required.

A task may be recoverable even when many underlying states remain possible.

10. Exact Task Sufficiency

Let

\[ T: X \longrightarrow W \]

be the task of interest.

The observation \(r\) is sufficient for \(T\) when there exists

\[ \widetilde T: Y \longrightarrow W \]

such that

\[ \boxed{ T = \widetilde T \circ r }. \]

This means the task can be computed entirely from the observed representation.

11. Fibre Criterion for Exact Sufficiency

Exact sufficiency is equivalent to requiring that the task be constant on every observation fibre:

\[ r(x) = r(x') \] \[ \Longrightarrow \] \[ T(x) = T(x'). \]

Therefore information may be discarded safely whenever the discarded distinctions do not alter the required task.

Conversely, if

\[ r(x)=r(x') \quad\text{but}\quad T(x)\neq T(x'), \]

then the observation has destroyed information necessary for the task.

12. Completion-Fibre Task Criterion

Using the completion fibre, exact task determination requires

\[ T(x) = T(x') \qquad \forall x,x' \in \mathfrak C_A(\ell). \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ \left| T\left( \mathfrak C_A(\ell) \right) \right| = 1 }. \]

The completion fibre may therefore contain many states while still being perfectly sufficient for the task.

13. Approximate Sufficiency

Exact equality may be too strong in numerical, statistical, experimental, or physical settings.

Let \(d_W\) be a metric on task space \(W\).

A representation may be approximately sufficient at tolerance \(\varepsilon\) when

\[ d_W \left( T(x), T(x') \right) \le \varepsilon \]

whenever

\[ r(x)=r(x'). \]

Equivalently, one may require a reconstruction

\[ \widetilde T: Y \longrightarrow W \]

satisfying

\[ d_W \left( T(x), \widetilde T(r(x)) \right) \le \varepsilon. \]

The metric and tolerance must be stated explicitly.

14. Response Relations

A response need not always be represented by an ordinary function.

A general response relation may be written

\[ \mathcal R: X \rightrightarrows Y. \]

For a state \(x\), the admissible responses are

\[ \mathcal R(x) \subseteq Y. \]

A deterministic response is the special case

\[ \left| \mathcal R(x) \right| = 1 \]

for every admissible \(x\).

15. Continuation

Continuation asks whether the current representation contains enough information to support a required future response.

Suppose evolution is described by

\[ \Phi_t: X \longrightarrow X. \]

A future task at time \(t\) is

\[ T_t = T \circ \Phi_t. \]

Observation \(r\) remains sufficient for that future task if there exists

\[ \widetilde T_t: Y \longrightarrow W \]

satisfying

\[ \boxed{ T \circ \Phi_t = \widetilde T_t \circ r }. \]

16. Continuation Failure

Suppose two states are currently observationally indistinguishable:

\[ r(x) = r(x'). \]

If evolution produces

\[ T(\Phi_t(x)) \neq T(\Phi_t(x')), \]

then the current representation is insufficient for the future task.

The missing distinction may have been irrelevant at the observation time but becomes relevant after evolution.

17. Regimes

System laws may depend on an active regime.

Let

\[ \lambda \in \Lambda \]

denote a regime parameter.

Evolution may then take the form

\[ \Phi_t^{(\lambda)}: X \longrightarrow X. \]

Different regimes may therefore induce different continuation conditions, admissible responses, or required distinctions.

Regime information is part of the law context and should not be silently omitted when it changes the system response.

18. Law Passports

A response problem may require not only state information but information identifying the law or regime governing the state.

Schematically, one may treat the effective response context as

\[ (x,\lambda) \in X\times\Lambda. \]

A law Passport records the law-context distinctions necessary for lawful interpretation or continuation.

Thus two identical state descriptions may still require different responses if they belong to different active regimes.

19. Constraints

Let the admissible state set be

\[ K \subseteq X. \]

A lawful response may require

\[ x\in K. \]

Under evolution, viability may require

\[ \Phi_t(x) \in K \qquad \forall t \text{ in the required interval}. \]

Constraints therefore affect both the available state space and the admissible continuation.

20. Interventions

Let

\[ u \in U \]

denote an intervention or control.

A controlled evolution may be represented by

\[ \Phi_t: X\times U \longrightarrow X. \]

or more generally by

\[ \Phi_t^{(\lambda,u)} \]

when both regime and intervention matter.

A representation that is sufficient without intervention may become insufficient once interventions alter which distinctions matter.

21. Response Under Intervention

Suppose a task depends on both state and intervention:

\[ T: X\times U \longrightarrow W. \]

If only \(r(x)\) is retained, exact intervention-aware sufficiency requires a map

\[ \widetilde T: Y\times U \longrightarrow W \]

such that

\[ \boxed{ T(x,u) = \widetilde T(r(x),u) }. \]

22. Obstruction

An obstruction occurs when the currently retained information does not determine the response required by the task.

For an observation value \(y\), define the task image of its fibre:

\[ T(r^{-1}(y)) = \left\{ T(x): x\in r^{-1}(y) \right\}. \]

Exact recovery fails whenever

\[ \left| T(r^{-1}(y)) \right| > 1. \]

The spread of possible task values inside one observational fibre is a direct mathematical signature of task obstruction.

23. Repair

Repair introduces additional information sufficient to eliminate an identified obstruction.

Suppose an additional observation is

\[ q: X \longrightarrow Z. \]

The repaired observation becomes

\[ (r,q): X \longrightarrow Y\times Z. \]

Repair succeeds for task \(T\) when

\[ (r,q)(x) = (r,q)(x') \] \[ \Longrightarrow \] \[ T(x) = T(x'). \]

Thus repair does not require reconstructing every lost detail.

It requires restoring enough information for the task.

24. Minimal Repair Question

What is GURF?

GURF is a mathematical framework for studying lawful system responses.

It asks which distinctions in an underlying state are necessary for a task, and whether observation, compression, constraints, evolution, or intervention preserve enough information for lawful continuation.

Branch Mathematics provides the broader meta-structural mathematics. GURF specializes that machinery to systems with genuine response semantics.

Core Questions

Required Information

Which distinctions in the underlying system must remain available for the current or future task?

Observation and Compression

Does an observation or reduced representation preserve the information required for later response?

Continuation

Can the system continue lawfully from the information that remains?

Obstruction

What missing distinction, constraint, or structural incompatibility prevents the required response?

Repair

What additional information or lawful structural change is sufficient to restore the required continuation?

Response Structure

GURF may represent states, observations, interventions, constraints, regimes, tasks, continuations, and responses using explicitly typed mathematical structures.

Exact, stochastic, nondeterministic, approximate, and other response semantics must remain distinguished when their mathematical laws differ.

Relationship to Branch Mathematics

Branch Mathematics governs domain-independent representation, transformation, composition, interfaces, realization, and re-entry.

GURF is invoked when a problem specifically concerns system response, information sufficiency, interventions, observations, constraints, regimes, or continuation.

Research Status

GURF is an active mathematical research framework under continuing formalization, proof development, testing, comparison with existing mathematics, and application research.

Mathematical validity, novelty, physical interpretation, and external validation are treated as separate questions.
