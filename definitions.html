Definitions

Formal reference for Branch Mathematics and GURF. Each major definition states what the object is, what mathematical data it carries, what it may do, what does not follow from the definition, how proposed instances can fail, and where to see the object used in worked examples.

1. How to Use This Reference

A BM definition names a mathematical role and the obligations attached to that role. It does not replace the native mathematics of the object being used.

Read each definition in two directions. First ask what information and structure the object carries. Then ask what a later calculation is allowed to do with it. That second question is where domains, Passports, Interfaces, composition, coherence, realization, export, and re-entry become important.

Student rule

Do the native mathematics correctly first. BM then records how distinct pieces of that mathematics participate together and which obligations must remain visible.

5. Branch Mathematics

Branch Mathematics
Status: canonical framework definition
Definition

Branch Mathematics is meta-structural mathematics of lawful representation and transformation. It studies how distinct mathematical structures can participate in one problem while retaining the types, laws, distinctions, admissible states, operations, interfaces, and native notions of validity that make each structure mathematically lawful.

\[ \boxed{ \text{lawful Branches} \longrightarrow \text{lawful Interactions} \longrightarrow \text{combined computation} \longrightarrow \text{Response} \longrightarrow \begin{cases} \text{terminal result},\\[3pt] \text{Root if downstream admissible}. \end{cases} } \]

Characteristics

  • Meta-structural rather than domain-replacing.
  • Typed rather than symbolically unrestricted.
  • Admissibility-sensitive.
  • Distinction-preserving when downstream mathematics requires a distinction.
  • Sector-correct: Set, algebraic, topological, probabilistic, operator, geometric, PDE, computational, and other realizations retain their own semantics.
Abilities
  • Organize several mathematical structures in one problem.
  • Specify lawful Interfaces and typed paths.
  • Audit transformation, composition, quotienting, coherence, realization, export, and re-entry.
  • Track which information must survive for later mathematics.
  • Organize Trees, Responses, Roots, Networks, and Configurations.
Limits
  • BM does not replace native proof.
  • BM does not make an invalid theorem valid.
  • BM does not authorize arbitrary cross-domain interaction.
  • BM does not establish physical realizability merely from formal consistency.
  • BM need not be invoked when it adds no genuine mathematical leverage.
Failure modes
  • Unspecified or incompatible types.
  • Domain mismatch.
  • Lost downstream-required distinctions.
  • Illicit promotion of a sector-specific identity into a universal rule.
  • Unverified interface, coherence, realization, or re-entry.
Sector scope. BM is deliberately multi-sector. The native sector supplies the actual mathematical meaning of its maps, objects, equivalences, limits, and admissibility conditions.
Worked examples
Integrated teaching example Full system Research scale

6. GURF

GURF
Status: canonical response-specialized framework under BM governance
Definition

GURF is the response-specialized framework used when the problem contains essential response semantics: states, responses, observations, Tasks, Interventions, Constraints, Regimes, ambiguity, recovery, continuation, or future response requirements.

\[ \boxed{ \text{Which distinctions in the underlying state are necessary for the required response or future Task?} } \]
Abilities
  • Audit Task sufficiency.
  • Detect task-relevant information loss.
  • Separate response from observation.
  • Track intervention and operation order.
  • Represent ambiguity, inverse fibres, obstruction, repair, and continuation.
  • Separate static sufficiency from dynamic closure.
Limits
  • GURF is not required when response semantics are absent.
  • GURF does not replace native mathematics.
  • Observation equivalence does not imply physical identity.
  • Information loss does not by itself imply causal influence.
  • Deterministic formulas do not automatically extend unchanged to stochastic, quantum, or nondeterministic sectors.
Worked examples
Task sufficiency Dynamic closure Full response system

7. Branch

Branch
Status: canonical BM structural role
Definition

A Branch is a typed mathematical carrier of information, distinctions, structure, and lawful mathematical behavior. A Branch is specified strongly enough that its admissible states, native laws, operations, and Interfaces can be checked rather than guessed.

\[ \boxed{ B= \left( \mathsf S_B, \tau_B, X_B, A_B, \mathcal L_B, \mathcal O_B, \mathcal P_B \right) } \] where \[ \begin{aligned} \mathsf S_B&=\text{mathematical sector},\\ \tau_B&=\text{declared type},\\ X_B&=\text{carrier or state object},\\ A_B&=\text{admissible domain or sector-correct analogue},\\ \mathcal L_B&=\text{laws, identities, constraints, invariants},\\ \mathcal O_B&=\text{admissible native operations or morphisms},\\ \mathcal P_B&=\text{Passport and Interface obligations}. \end{aligned} \]

Characteristics

  • A Branch retains a mathematical identity independently of any later Interaction.
  • The carrier alone does not determine the Branch.
  • Two Branches may use isomorphic or identical carriers while having different types, laws, or admissibility.
  • A Branch may be scalar, vectorial, functional, algebraic, geometric, probabilistic, operator-valued, combinatorial, computational, or another mathematically specified type.
Abilities
  • Participate in admissible Interactions.
  • Supply information from which Leaves are extracted.
  • Participate in Trees, Networks, and Configurations.
  • Receive compatible exported or Root-derived information.
  • Support native Operations.
Limits
  • The presence of two Branches does not imply that a lawful Interaction between them exists.
  • Declaring a Branch does not establish physical truth or realizability.
  • The seven-field record is a BM organizational specification; the native sector supplies the actual internal structure.
Failure modes
  • Carrier supplied without a usable type.
  • Admissible set inconsistent with the native laws.
  • Operations listed that are not defined on the declared domain.
  • Passport or Interface obligations omitted when later use depends on them.
Teaching note

A vector, matrix, function, graph, or probability law becomes a BM Branch because of the complete mathematical role it carries, not because BM renames the underlying object.

8. Branch Information

Branch Information
Status: canonical BM interpretation
Definition

Branch Information is the information and distinction structure carried by a Branch. A distinction is relevant when the Branch's native mathematics treats two admissible states as different in a way that matters to a declared operation, law, Interface, or downstream Task.

\[ x\neq_B x' \] denotes a distinction recognized by Branch \(B\).
Limits
  • Not every distinction in an underlying model must be preserved by every representation.
  • Task-relative reduction may lawfully merge distinctions that are irrelevant to the declared later mathematics.
Worked examples
Same carrier, different Branch Information loss

9. Interaction

Interaction
Status: canonical BM structural role
Definition

An Interaction is a lawful typed mathematical action in which one or more Branches participate, with multi-Branch joint action as the central case.

\[ I: D_I \subseteq A_{B_1}\times\cdots\times A_{B_m} \longrightarrow Z_I \] in a deterministic Set-like realization.

Characteristics

  • Typed Branch participation.
  • Explicit admissible domain.
  • Declared output type.
  • Compatibility with participating native laws.
  • Possible dependence on order, Regime, context, or Interface data.
Abilities
  • Combine Branch information.
  • Produce intermediate mathematical structure.
  • Expose one or many Leaves.
  • Participate in Trees and Networks.
Limits
  • Formal writability does not imply admissibility.
  • An Interaction need not be commutative, associative, total, invertible, or information-preserving.
  • The deterministic function form is not universal.
Failure modes
  • Empty or violated interaction domain.
  • Incompatible Branch types.
  • Missing unit, regularity, positivity, conservation, or other native requirement.
  • Using deterministic semantics where the native interaction is stochastic, relational, or quantum.
Sector scope. Functions are appropriate in deterministic Set-like sectors; kernels, relations, operator maps, channels, instruments, bilinear forms, differential operators, or other sector-correct constructions may be required elsewhere.
Worked examples
Power Interaction Force Interaction Bayesian Interaction Matrix Interaction

10. Leaf

Leaf
Status: canonical BM structural role
Definition

A Leaf is typed mathematical information extracted from an Interaction result. The extraction map is part of the definition of the Leaf's provenance.

\[ z=I(x_1,\ldots,x_m), \qquad L_j=\ell_j(z). \]

Characteristics

  • One Interaction may expose several different Leaves.
  • Different Leaves may retain different aspects of the same Interaction.
  • A Leaf records extracted interacting information rather than an arbitrary intermediate symbol.
Abilities
  • Feed a later Interaction when the receiving domain and Interface admit it.
  • Support diagnostics, reductions, decisions, corrections, invariants, or later derivations.
  • Be combined with other Leaves or Branch information.
Limits
  • A Leaf is not automatically a Root.
  • A Leaf is not automatically an independent Branch.
  • A Leaf's existence does not waive the domain and Passport checks of later Operations.
Failure modes
  • Extraction map undefined on the Interaction result.
  • Extracted value loses a distinction required by its declared later use.
  • Later Interaction accepts a different type, unit, regularity class, or semantic object.
Teaching note

The examples deliberately show Leaves being used to derive new Leaves. That is where the role becomes useful rather than decorative.

11. Tree

Tree
Status: canonical BM structural role
Definition

A Tree is an organized mathematical structure coordinating Branches, Interactions, Leaves, Passports, Interfaces, and intermediate computation in order to produce a Response.

\[ \mathcal T = \bigl( \mathcal B,\mathcal I,\mathcal L,\mathcal P,\mathcal F,\mathcal C \bigr) \] is a useful organizational form, not a universal intrinsic tuple.

Characteristics

  • A Tree can be sequential, branching, parallel, coupled, recursive, or organized by more general categorical, graph, operator, or limit constructions.
  • A Tree is distinct from its Response.
  • One Network may contain several Trees.
Abilities
  • Coordinate several Branches and Interactions.
  • Carry Leaves into later lawful calculations.
  • Expose path-coherence and Interface obligations.
  • Produce a typed Response.
Limits
  • A BM Tree need not be a literal graph-theoretic tree.
  • A Tree structure alone does not establish coherence or realizability.
  • Not every problem needs a Tree.
Worked examples
Sequential Tree Branching Tree Parallel Tree Coupled Tree Full Tree

12. Response

Response
Status: canonical BM structural role; central GURF role
Definition

A Response is the typed output produced by a Tree or another organized BM computation. A Response records what the current computation produced; it does not by itself certify that another mathematical structure may use the result.

\[ \mathcal T(x)\longmapsto R. \]
Abilities
  • Serve as a terminal result.
  • Be tested against a downstream Interface.
  • Become a Root when the required receiving admissibility is established.
Limits
  • Response does not imply Root.
  • Response does not imply physical response unless the native problem is physical.
  • Response does not imply recurrence or re-entry.
Worked examples
Controller Response Integrated Response

13. Root

Root
Status: canonical BM structural role
Definition

A Root is a Response or resulting mathematical structure whose required downstream admissibility has been established for a specified receiving mathematical context.

\[ \boxed{ \text{Root role} = \text{Response} + \text{verified downstream admissibility}. } \]

Characteristics

  • Root status is relative to the receiving mathematics.
  • A Root may be scalar, vector, matrix, function, probability law, operator, structured object, or another admitted type.
  • A terminal Response need not become a Root.
Abilities
  • Enter compatible downstream Branches or Interactions.
  • Support re-entry, attachment, or further derivation.
  • Carry required Passport information into later mathematics.
Limits
  • Root does not automatically imply recurrence.
  • Root does not automatically imply conservation, invertibility, uniqueness, or physical realization.
  • The same Response may be a Root for one receiver and not another.
Failure modes
  • Receiving-domain failure.
  • Missing unit, type, law, or Passport.
  • Using a Response before the downstream gate is checked.
Worked examples
Basic Root Scalar Root Vector Root Matrix Root Function Root Receiver-relative Root

14. Rooting

Rooting
Status: active candidate BM structure
Definition

Rooting is a candidate BM operation in which a Root or compatible Root-derived law family is attached to an admissible interior location of a receiving Branch or structure so that further mathematics can develop from that attachment.

\[ \rho: (R,a,B)\longmapsto B' \] schematically, where \(a\) is an admissible attachment site and \(B'\) is the resulting receiving structure.
Limits
  • The existence of a Root does not guarantee an admissible attachment site.
  • Rooting is not defined as unrestricted graph insertion or recursive self-reference.
Failure modes
  • Type or Interface mismatch at the attachment site.
  • Required Passport transfer fails.
  • The resulting Branch violates native laws.
Worked examples
Basic Rooting Re-entry context

Rebranching

Rebranching is candidate BM language for the new lawful Branch development that begins from an accepted attachment or Rooting site.

Limits

16. Passport

Passport
Status: canonical BM structure; central GURF mechanism
Definition

A Passport is declared information that must remain available, verifiable, or compatible at an Interface or later stage. Passport content may include distinctions, invariants, units, law context, observations, uncertainty, coupling requirements, Task requirements, conservation data, or other downstream obligations.

\[ \mathcal P = \{p_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in A} \] is a generic family of Passport requirements. Their native mathematical type need not be Boolean.

Characteristics

  • Task-relative and Interface-relative.
  • May be exact, approximate, relational, probabilistic, operator-valued, or otherwise sector-correct.
  • May combine several independent requirements.
Abilities
  • Prevent unsafe information loss.
  • Control export and re-entry.
  • Track law and Regime context.
  • Certify required distinctions for later Tasks.
Limits
  • A Passport does not prove the native theorem whose information it records.
  • No universal Boolean predicate representation is imposed.
Failure modes
  • Required information discarded.
  • Coupling or witness scope silently changed.
  • Law or Regime metadata omitted.
  • Approximation used without a declared tolerance.
Worked examples
Basic Passport Task Passport Failure Approximate Full system

19. Passport Equivalence

Status: exact deterministic equivalence induced by a Passport map
Definition

Two states are Passport-equivalent when the declared Passport representation cannot distinguish them.

\[ x\sim_{\mathcal P}x' \iff B_A(x)=B_A(x'). \]
Limits
  • This is a deterministic Set-like realization; other sectors require their native equivalence or comparison semantics.

20. Fibre

Fibre
Status: standard mathematical construction used in BM/GURF
Definition

For a map \(r:X\to Y\), the fibre over \(y\in r(X)\) is the set of source states represented by that same value.

\[ r^{-1}(y)=\{x\in X:r(x)=y\}. \]
Worked examples
Sensor fibre Finite fibres

21. Completion Fibre

Completion Fibre
Status: canonical GURF exact-set construction
Definition

Given several Passport observations, the Completion Fibre is the set of source states simultaneously consistent with the observed Passport data.

\[ \mathfrak C_A(\ell) = \bigcap_{\alpha\in A} b_\alpha^{-1}(\ell_\alpha). \]
Limits
  • An empty Completion Fibre signals inconsistency under the declared exact model.
  • No probability distribution over the fibre is implied.

22. Task

Task
Status: canonical GURF concept
Definition

A Task is a declared mathematical quantity, decision, property, control requirement, or future-use requirement whose determination matters to the problem.

\[ T:X\to W \]
Limits
  • Task choice is part of the problem specification.
  • Sufficiency for one Task does not imply sufficiency for another.

23. Exact Sufficiency

Exact Sufficiency
Status: exact deterministic factorization concept
Definition

A representation \(r:X\to Y\) is exactly sufficient for a Task \(T:X\to W\) when the Task factors through the representation.

\[ \boxed{T=\widetilde T\circ r} \] for some \(\widetilde T:r(X)\to W\). Equivalently, \[ r(x)=r(x')\Rightarrow T(x)=T(x') \] in deterministic Set semantics.
Limits
  • Sufficiency is Task-relative.
  • Static exact sufficiency does not imply dynamic closure.
  • The Set criterion is not silently reused in other semantic sectors.
Worked examples
Exact sufficiency Finite audit

24. Task Equivalence

Task Equivalence
Status: exact deterministic equivalence
Definition

Two states are Task-equivalent when the declared Task assigns them the same Task value.

\[ x\sim_Tx'\iff T(x)=T(x'). \]

Sufficiency Relation

\[ \sim_r\;\subseteq\;\sim_T. \]

26. Approximate Sufficiency

Approximate Sufficiency
Status: sector-dependent quantitative concept
Definition

Approximate Sufficiency replaces exact Task equality by a declared error, risk, divergence, norm, metric, probability, or operational tolerance.

\[ d_W\!\left(T(x),\widehat T(r(x))\right)\le\varepsilon \] is one metric realization.
Limits

\[ x\text{ admissible}\iff x\in A \] for a Set-like admissible set \(A\subseteq X\).

37. Path Coherence

Path Coherence
Status: active BM concept
Definition

Path Coherence asks whether two lawful paths with common source and destination agree according to the destination's declared notion of equivalence.

\[ \Delta_{p,q} = \{x\in D_p\cap D_q:F_p(x)\not\simeq_DF_q(x)\}. \] Exact path coherence holds when \(\Delta_{p,q}=\varnothing\).
Limits
  • Coherence is checked only on the shared domain.
  • Equality, isomorphism, gauge equivalence, equality in distribution, and numerical tolerance are not interchangeable.
Worked examples
Parallel conversions Calibration defect Shared-domain coherence

Limits

46. Gluing

Gluing
Status: standard mathematical idea used within BM; sector-dependent

64. Seed

Seed
Status: candidate BM primitive
Definition

A Seed is information assigned a lawful generative role that does not yet directly export a Root. It generates Seed states until a declared export condition is met.

\[ S_0\xrightarrow{G}S_1\xrightarrow{G}S_2\to\cdots \xrightarrow{\text{export condition}}R. \]

Characteristics

  • Optional rather than universal.
  • Defined by generative role, not temporal firstness.
  • May be scalar, vector, parameter, subspace generator, or another lawful object.
Limits
  • Initial data is not automatically a Seed.
  • Seed language does not imply biological agency or intention.

Seed State

Limits

66. Seed Export Condition

Seed Export Condition
Status: candidate BM concept
Definition

A Seed Export Condition is the declared mathematical criterion determining when generative Seed-state information may be exported as a Response for downstream Root testing.

Task Diameter

Task Diameter measures the spread of Task values over an ambiguity or Completion Fibre under a declared metric.

Law Space

A Law Space is a mathematical carrier of possible laws, Regimes, parameter families, or rule contexts when the active law itself must be typed and transformed.

72. Conserved Quantity

Conserved Quantity
Status: native-domain mathematical concept
Definition

For an evolution \(\Phi_t:X\to X\), a quantity \(C:X\to V\) is conserved when it is invariant along the stated evolution.

Limits
  • BM may track or preserve an independently established conservation law; it does not create one.

73. Mathematical Realizability

Mathematical Realizability
Status: canonical BM distinction
Definition

Mathematical Realizability asks whether the stated abstract object or construction exists in the claimed mathematical category or sector with all required structure.

Limits

74. Physical Realizability

Physical Realizability

Physical Realizability asks whether a mathematically specified structure can be instantiated by a physical system under the relevant empirical laws, scales, materials, causal constraints, and experimental conditions.

Limits
  • Mathematical realizability is necessary in many applications but does not by itself establish physical realizability.

80. UKE

UKE
Status: stable canonical GURF extension
Definition

UKE contains kernel-specific response mathematics while keeping distinct meanings of the word kernel typed: nullspace, integral kernel, Green kernel, resolvent, reproducing kernel, spectral kernel, memory kernel, and related objects.

\[ \ker F=\{x:F(x)=0\}, \qquad (Tf)(x)=\int K(x,y)f(y)\,dy, \] \[ L_xG(x,x')=\delta(x-x'), \qquad R(z)=(L-zI)^{-1} \] where each expression is defined.
Limits
  • A chosen kernel is not automatically a physically derived response mechanism; origin, boundary conditions, causality, and admissibility remain separate questions.

81. GRDE

GRDE
Status: frozen at GURF-GRDE-008
Definition

GRDE is the frozen diagrammatic response-representation extension. It represents response fibres, carriers, provenance, cuts, fusion, and related response structure when a diagrammatic calculus is useful.

Limits
  • GRDE does not replace the underlying mathematics.
  • No diagrammatic object is presumed unless its formal reconstruction is defined.

82. HDE

HDE
Status: active canonical GURF extension
Definition

HDE handles response problems whose relevant response domain extends beyond a narrowly localized source or primary region, including extended support, domain propagation, boundary transfer, persistence, and observation.

\[ D\subseteq D_{\rm ext}, \qquad H=D_{\rm ext}\setminus D \] is one Set-like working decomposition.
Limits
  • HDE is not restricted to one physical halo theory.
  • The propagation law is native-domain specific.
  • Nested spatial sets are not universally required.

83. GHSE

GHSE
Status: active canonical GURF extension
Definition

GHSE studies response states whose behavior depends on lawful joining, attachment, holding, persistence, release, or transition among coupled components.

\[ \mathcal H\subseteq X_A\times X_B, \qquad J:D_J\subseteq X_A\times X_B\to X_{AB} \] are possible realizations.
Limits
  • Arbitrary joining is not admissible.
  • Hold criteria may be relational, geometric, probabilistic, dynamical, or otherwise native to the problem.

HEC
Status: BM-owned calculus; not a GURF extension
Definition

HEC is BM-owned domain-independent project mathematics. GURF may use HEC when response problems require it, but GURF use does not change HEC ownership.

Limits
  • No additional HEC axiom or theorem is inferred merely from its placement in BM.

90. Portability Firewall

Portability Firewall
Status: canonical BM/GURF ownership rule
Definition

The Portability Firewall assigns domain-independent mathematical kernels to BM and retains machinery under GURF when its identity essentially depends on response semantics.

\[ \text{domain-independent kernel}\longrightarrow\text{BM}, \] \[ \text{essential response identity}\longrightarrow\text{GURF}. \]

  • Ownership is determined by mathematical identity, not by where a construct happened to be used first.

93. BM and GURF Distinction

BM and GURF Distinction
Status: canonical architecture distinction
Definition

BM is the broader meta-structural mathematics of lawful representation and transformation. GURF is the response-specialized mathematics used when response semantics become essential.

\[ \boxed{\mathrm{BM}=\text{broader meta-structural layer}}, \qquad \boxed{\mathrm{GURF}=\text{response-specialized layer under BM governance}}. \]
Limits
  • BM need not be rewritten as a response theory.
  • GURF need not be invoked merely because BM vocabulary appears.

94. GURF Architecture

GURF Architecture
Status: current canonical architecture
Definition

The active GURF architecture consists of the Core together with the response-specialized extensions SOE, ACE, IRE, UKE, GRDE, HDE, and GHSE.

\[ \mathrm{GURF} = \mathrm{Core} +\mathrm{SOE} +\mathrm{ACE} +\mathrm{IRE} +\mathrm{UKE} +\mathrm{GRDE}_{\rm frozen} +\mathrm{HDE} +\mathrm{GHSE}. \]
Limits
  • Extensions are activated by need; the sum is architectural notation, not vector-space addition.

95. Governing Definition Rule

Governing Definition Rule
Status: canonical rigor rule
Definition

A BM/GURF definition specifies a role and its mathematical obligations. Consequences, existence, uniqueness, novelty, physical interpretation, and realizability require separate arguments appropriate to those claims.

\[ \boxed{\text{definition}\neq\text{theorem}\neq\text{model}\neq\text{realization claim}}. \]
Teaching note

This rule keeps the definitions useful to students: learn the role here, prove its consequences in the theorem registry, and study its instantiated mathematics in the examples and research pages.
