Passports

Formal mathematical structures for specifying which distinctions, observations, constraints, invariants, admissibility conditions, and compatibility requirements must survive across a mathematical interface.

1. What a Passport Is

A Passport specifies the information and mathematical conditions that must remain available for a representation, transformation, interaction, export, Root attachment, or response to remain lawful.

Passports are not permission labels.

They are mathematical specifications.

A Passport may describe:

observations · preserved distinctions · admissible states · invariants · constraints · interface requirements · task requirements · law context · quantifiers · approximation tolerances · coupling requirements

2. General Working Form

A useful general representation of a Passport is

\[ P = \left( X, \mathcal C, \{b_c\}_{c\in\mathcal C}, \mathcal A \right), \]

where:

Object Meaning
\(X\) underlying mathematical state or object space
\(\mathcal C\) index set of Passport conditions or observations
\(b_c:X\to Y_c\) observation, invariant, test, or distinction map
\(\mathcal A\) admissibility, compatibility, law, or interface requirements

This tuple is a general working form. Individual mathematical categories may require additional or different structure.

3. Passport Observation Map

A family of Passport observations

\[ b_c: X \longrightarrow Y_c, \qquad c\in\mathcal C \]

can be combined into one product observation

\[ B_P: X \longrightarrow \prod_{c\in\mathcal C}Y_c \]

defined by

\[ \boxed{ B_P(x) = \left( b_c(x) \right)_{c\in\mathcal C}. } \]

The combined Passport therefore records every active Passport observation simultaneously.

4. Raw Passport Equivalence

Passport observations induce an observational equivalence relation.

\[ \boxed{ x \sim_P x' \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b_c(x) = b_c(x') \quad \forall c\in\mathcal C. } \]

Equivalently,

\[ x\sim_P x' \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad B_P(x)=B_P(x'). \]

States in the same Passport equivalence class are indistinguishable under the active Passport observations.

5. Passport Equivalence Is an Equivalence Relation

When the observation maps \(b_c\) are ordinary well-defined functions, \(\sim_P\) is automatically an equivalence relation.

Reflexivity

\[ b_c(x)=b_c(x) \]

for every \(c\), hence

\[ x\sim_P x. \]

Symmetry

\[ x\sim_P x' \Longrightarrow b_c(x)=b_c(x') \]

and therefore

\[ b_c(x')=b_c(x), \]

giving

\[ x'\sim_P x. \]

Transitivity

If

\[ x\sim_P x' \quad\text{and}\quad x'\sim_P x'', \]

then

\[ b_c(x) = b_c(x') = b_c(x'') \]

for every \(c\), hence

\[ x\sim_P x''. \]

6. Passport Classes

The equivalence class of \(x\) is

\[ [x]_P = \left\{ x'\in X: x'\sim_P x \right\}. \]

The Passport quotient set is

\[ X/{\sim_P}. \]

Each element of the quotient is a collection of states that the Passport does not distinguish.

7. Passport Fibres

Let

\[ \ell = (\ell_c)_{c\in\mathcal C} \]

be a Passport observation value.

Its Passport fibre is

\[ \boxed{ \mathfrak C_P(\ell) = B_P^{-1}(\ell). } \]

Expanding the product observation gives

\[ \boxed{ \mathfrak C_P(\ell) = \bigcap_{c\in\mathcal C} b_c^{-1}(\ell_c). } \]

This is the set of every underlying state compatible with all active Passport observations.

8. Empty, Singleton, and Nontrivial Fibres

Inconsistent Passport data

\[ \mathfrak C_P(\ell) = \varnothing. \]

No state satisfies every Passport condition simultaneously.

Unique completion

\[ \left| \mathfrak C_P(\ell) \right| = 1. \]

The Passport data determines one compatible state.

Nontrivial completion

\[ \left| \mathfrak C_P(\ell) \right| > 1. \]

Multiple underlying states remain possible.

This is not automatically a defect. The remaining ambiguity may be irrelevant to the required task.

9. Task Sufficiency

Let the required task be

\[ T: X \longrightarrow W. \]

Passport \(P\) is sufficient for \(T\) when there exists

\[ \widetilde T: B_P(X) \longrightarrow W \]

satisfying

\[ \boxed{ T = \widetilde T \circ B_P. } \]

The task can then be computed using only Passport information.

10. Fibre Criterion for Passport Sufficiency

Passport sufficiency is equivalent to

\[ B_P(x)=B_P(x') \] \[ \Longrightarrow \] \[ T(x)=T(x'). \]

Using Passport equivalence,

\[ \boxed{ x\sim_P x' \Longrightarrow T(x)=T(x'). } \]

Therefore the Passport may discard distinctions only when those distinctions do not alter the required task.

11. Equivalence-Relation Form of Sufficiency

Define task equivalence by

\[ x \sim_T x' \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T(x)=T(x'). \]

Then Passport sufficiency is exactly

\[ \boxed{ \sim_P \subseteq \sim_T. } \]

Every identification made by the Passport must also be an identification that the task is willing to make.

12. Passport Refinement

Let \(P\) and \(Q\) be two Passports on the same state space.

\(P\) is at least as informative as \(Q\) when

\[ \boxed{ \sim_P \subseteq \sim_Q. } \]

A finer Passport identifies fewer states.

A coarser Passport identifies more states.

Thus Passport systems naturally admit an information-refinement ordering.

13. Combined Passports

Suppose \(P\) and \(Q\) use observation maps

\[ B_P:X\to Y \]

and

\[ B_Q:X\to Z. \]

Their joint observation is

\[ (B_P,B_Q): X \longrightarrow Y\times Z. \]

The joint equivalence relation satisfies

\[ x \sim_{P\wedge Q} x' \] \[ \Longleftrightarrow \] \[ x\sim_P x' \quad\text{and}\quad x\sim_Q x'. \]

Hence

\[ \sim_{P\wedge Q} = \sim_P \cap \sim_Q. \]

14. Combined Passport Fibres

If Passport \(P\) gives observation \(y\) and Passport \(Q\) gives observation \(z\), then

\[ \boxed{ \mathfrak C_{P\wedge Q}(y,z) = \mathfrak C_P(y) \cap \mathfrak C_Q(z). } \]

Adding Passport information therefore refines the candidate state set by intersection.

15. Joint Feasibility Is Stronger Than Separate Feasibility

Suppose condition \(c\) has feasible set

\[ P_c \subseteq X. \]

Separate feasibility means

\[ P_c\neq\varnothing \qquad \forall c. \]

Joint feasibility requires

\[ \boxed{ \bigcap_{c\in\mathcal C} P_c \neq \varnothing. } \]

Each Passport condition can be individually satisfiable while the whole Passport remains impossible.

16. Witness Reuse

Quantifier scope matters.

The statement

\[ \forall c \; \exists x_c \in P_c \]

does not imply

\[ \exists x \; \forall c, \quad x\in P_c. \]

The first allows a different witness for every condition.

The second requires one common witness.

A Passport must state which semantics is intended.

17. Passport Quotients

The raw Passport quotient is

\[ X/{\sim_P}. \]

But forming a set-theoretic quotient does not prove that every operation on \(X\) descends to the quotient.

This distinction is central to structured Passport mathematics.

18. Operation Compatibility

Let

\[ \sigma: X^n \longrightarrow X \]

be an admissible operation.

To define

\[ \overline{\sigma} \left( [x_1]_P,\ldots,[x_n]_P \right) = \left[ \sigma(x_1,\ldots,x_n) \right]_P, \]

the result must be independent of representatives.

Therefore one requires

\[ x_i \sim_P y_i \qquad \forall i \] \[ \Longrightarrow \] \[ \boxed{ \sigma(x_1,\ldots,x_n) \sim_P \sigma(y_1,\ldots,y_n). } \]

19. Congruence Condition

In a finitary algebra

\[ A=(X,\Sigma), \]

Passport equivalence must be compatible with every operation whose structure is intended to survive the quotient.

That is,

\[ \forall \sigma\in\Sigma, \] \[ x_i\sim_P y_i \quad \forall i \] \[ \Longrightarrow \] \[ \sigma(x_1,\ldots,x_n) \sim_P \sigma(y_1,\ldots,y_n). \]

Under this condition, \(\sim_P\) is a congruence for the algebraic structure being preserved.

20. Why Raw Passport Equivalence May Fail

A Passport may correctly describe current observations while still being too coarse for later mathematics.

Suppose

\[ x\sim_P x'. \]

If a later admissible operation gives

\[ \sigma(x) \not\sim_P \sigma(x'), \]

then the operation can detect a distinction that the raw Passport quotient removed.

Observational equivalence is not automatically structural equivalence.

21. Structured Passport Quotient

A lawful structured quotient therefore requires two separate checks:

Observation condition

Which states are identified by the Passport?

Operation condition

Which mathematical operations remain well-defined after those identifications?

Only after compatibility is established can the relevant quotient structure be inherited lawfully.

22. Worked Example: A Simple Passport

Let

\[ X = \mathbb R^2. \]

Write states as

\[ x=(x_1,x_2). \]

Define Passport observation

\[ b(x_1,x_2) = x_1. \]

Then

\[ (x_1,x_2) \sim_P (y_1,y_2) \] \[ \Longleftrightarrow \] \[ x_1=y_1. \]

The Passport preserves the first coordinate and forgets the second.

23. Worked Example: A Task the Passport Preserves

Define

\[ T(x_1,x_2) = x_1^2. \]

If

\[ (x_1,x_2) \sim_P (y_1,y_2), \]

then

\[ x_1=y_1, \]

and therefore

\[ x_1^2=y_1^2. \]

So the Passport is sufficient for \(T\).

The induced task on Passport space is simply

\[ \widetilde T(u)=u^2. \]

Hence

\[ T = \widetilde T \circ b. \]
