Structural Registry

Formal mathematical objects and architectural structures used by Branch Mathematics to organize distinct mathematical branches, interactions, transformations, interfaces, responses, composition, realization, export, and re-entry.

1. Purpose of the Structural Registry

Branch Mathematics uses a typed structural architecture so that different mathematical objects can participate in one problem without losing the mathematical laws that distinguish them.

The structural registry records the principal object classes used by that architecture.

\[ \boxed{ \text{structure} = \text{objects} + \text{types} + \text{laws} + \text{interfaces} + \text{admissible operations} } \]

2. Branch

A Branch is a typed mathematical carrier of information, distinctions, structure, and lawful mathematical behavior.

A Branch does not replace the underlying mathematics.

It records the role that an existing mathematical object plays inside a BM construction.

\[ B = \left( X_B, \mathcal L_B, \mathcal O_B, P_B \right) \]

may be used as a general working form.

3. Branch Components

Component Meaning
\(X_B\) state, object, or carrier space
\(\mathcal L_B\) laws, identities, equations, or constraints
\(\mathcal O_B\) admissible mathematical operations
\(P_B\) Passport or interface-relevant information

Some Branches may require topology, measure, algebraic operations, differentiable structure, probability law, boundary data, or other additional structure.

4. Branch Type

Let

\[ \tau(B) \]

denote the mathematical type of Branch \(B\).

Examples include:

vector space · manifold · algebra · probability space · measure space · graph · Hilbert space · Banach space · PDE state space · operator family · finite state system · computational representation

Type information restricts which interactions and transformations are meaningful.

5. Branch State

A state carried by Branch \(B\) is written

\[ x_B \in X_B. \]

The state must satisfy whatever structural laws define admissibility in the Branch.

If the admissible subset is

\[ A_B \subseteq X_B, \]

then lawful Branch states satisfy

\[ x_B \in A_B. \]

6. Interaction

An Interaction is a lawful joint mathematical action involving two or more Branches.

For Branches

\[ B_1,\ldots,B_n, \]

an interaction may be represented as

\[ I: D_I \subseteq X_{B_1} \times \cdots \times X_{B_n} \longrightarrow Z_I. \]

7. Interaction Domain

The interaction domain

\[ D_I \]

records the combinations of Branch states that are actually admissible.

In general,

\[ D_I \neq X_{B_1} \times \cdots \times X_{B_n}. \]

Some formally writable combinations may violate constraints, typing, boundary conditions, conservation requirements, or other laws.

8. Interaction Output

For admissible input

\[ (x_1,\ldots,x_n) \in D_I, \]

the interaction produces

\[ z = I(x_1,\ldots,x_n). \]

The object \(z\) may later become a Leaf, Response, Root, intermediate state, exported object, or input to another interaction.

9. Leaf

A Leaf is mathematical information extracted from an interaction.

Let

\[ \ell_j: Z_I \longrightarrow L_j \]

be an extraction map.

Then

\[ \boxed{ L_j = \ell_j \left( I(x_1,\ldots,x_n) \right). } \]

10. Multiple Leaves

One interaction may produce multiple extracted quantities:

\[ \ell_1(z), \ell_2(z), \ldots, \ell_m(z). \]

These Leaves may carry different information and may have different relevance to later tasks.

A Leaf should therefore not be assumed to represent the full interaction output.

11. Leaf Sufficiency

Suppose a later task is

\[ T: Z_I \longrightarrow W. \]

Leaf \(\ell\) is sufficient for \(T\) when there exists

\[ \widetilde T: L \longrightarrow W \]

satisfying

\[ \boxed{ T = \widetilde T \circ \ell. } \]

12. Tree

A Tree is an organized collection of Branches and Interactions capable of producing a mathematical Response.

A Tree need not be graph-theoretically tree-shaped in every formal realization.

The term denotes the BM response-producing organization of Branches, Interactions, and derived information.

13. Linear Tree

A simple sequential Tree may have

\[ X_0 \xrightarrow{I_1} X_1 \xrightarrow{I_2} X_2 \xrightarrow{I_3} \cdots \xrightarrow{I_m} X_m. \]

Its overall response is

\[ \boxed{ \mathcal R_T = I_m \circ \cdots \circ I_2 \circ I_1. } \]

14. Nonlinear or Networked Tree

More general Trees may contain:

parallel branches · coupling · feedback · shared constraints · synchronization · conditional operations · iterative operations · multiple outputs · repeated Rooting · re-entry

Such structures may require more than ordinary sequential composition.

15. Response

A Response is the law-valued output produced by a Tree or other organized BM structure.

Abstractly,

\[ \mathcal R_T: D_T \longrightarrow Y_T. \]

For admissible state \(x\in D_T\),

\[ r = \mathcal R_T(x). \]

16. Response Versus Root

A Response becomes a Root when the result remains admissible as input to further lawful mathematical structure.

\[ \text{Response} \xrightarrow{\text{future admissibility}} \text{Root}. \]

This expression is process notation, not a reconstructed structural diagram.

17. Operation

An Operation is a typed lawful transformation.

A unary operation is

\[ O: D_O \subseteq X \longrightarrow Y. \]

An \(n\)-ary operation may be

\[ O: D_O \subseteq X_1\times\cdots\times X_n \longrightarrow Y. \]

18. Operation Contract

A BM operation should identify at least:

input type · output type · admissible domain · law requirements · Passport requirements · preserved quantities · failure conditions

In software terminology this resembles a contract, but the contract is mathematical rather than merely procedural.

19. Composition

Suppose

\[ F: X \longrightarrow Y \]

and

\[ G: Y \longrightarrow Z. \]

Then ordinary composition is

\[ G\circ F: X \longrightarrow Z. \]

BM additionally checks whether the intermediate representation preserves everything required by \(G\).

20. Typed Composability

A necessary condition for ordinary composition is

\[ \operatorname{cod}(F) = \operatorname{dom}(G) \]

or an explicitly defined compatible inclusion or conversion.

A type mismatch makes the composition ill-defined unless an additional lawful map resolves the mismatch.

21. Admissible Composition

Even when types match, admissibility may require

\[ F(x) \in D_G. \]

Thus the true domain of the composite is

\[ D_{G\circ F} = \left\{ x\in D_F: F(x)\in D_G \right\}. \]

22. Interface

An Interface is a typed compatibility structure through which mathematical information passes between Branches or representations.

Consider

\[ F: X_1 \longrightarrow X_2. \]

Let representations be

\[ P_1: X_1 \longrightarrow Q_1 \]

and

\[ P_2: X_2 \longrightarrow Q_2. \]

23. Exact Interface Compatibility

Suppose

\[ G: Q_1 \longrightarrow Q_2. \]

Exact compatibility is

\[ \boxed{ P_2\circ F = G\circ P_1. } \]

This states that the full transformation and represented transformation agree.

24. Approximate Interface Compatibility

If exact equality is inappropriate, let

\[ d: Q_2\times Q_2 \longrightarrow [0,\infty) \]

be an explicitly chosen metric or discrepancy.

One may require

\[ d \left( P_2(F(x)), G(P_1(x)) \right) \le \varepsilon. \]

The tolerance and metric are part of the mathematical specification.

25. Configuration

A Configuration records the active BM state at a given stage of a construction.

A general configuration may be written

\[ \mathcal X = \left( \mathcal B, \mathcal I, \mathcal L, \mathcal R, \mathcal P, \mathcal O, \mathcal A \right), \]

where the components may represent active Branches, Interactions, Leaves, Roots, Passports, Operations, and admissibility data.

26. Configuration State

A concrete configuration state may contain

\[ x_{\mathcal X} = \left( x_{B_1}, \ldots, x_{B_n}, r_1, \ldots, p_1, \ldots \right). \]

The exact structure depends on the problem.

27. Configuration Update

A BM update may be represented by

\[ U: \mathcal X \longrightarrow \mathcal X'. \]

An update may:

add a Branch · remove an inactive structure · produce a Leaf · create a Root · modify a Passport · apply an Operation · rebind an interface · update admissibility conditions

28. Transactional Operation

Some BM operations may be treated transactionally.

If operation \(O\) acts on configuration \(\mathcal X\), then

\[ O(\mathcal X) = \mathcal X' \]

only when all required checks succeed.

Otherwise the operation should be rejected or returned with an explicit failure state rather than silently producing an invalid configuration.

29. Network

A BM Network is a collection of connected Branches, Interactions, Roots, Passports, and Operations.

A simple graph-like representation may use

\[ \mathcal N = (V,E), \]

where vertices may represent mathematical structures and edges represent typed lawful relationships.

More general interactions may require hypergraphs or category-like structures.

30. Hyperinteraction

An interaction involving several Branches simultaneously may be modeled using a hyperedge

\[ e = \{B_1,\ldots,B_k\}. \]

The associated operation may be

\[ I_e: D_e \subseteq \prod_{j=1}^{k}X_{B_j} \longrightarrow Z_e. \]

31. Typed Path

A path through a BM network may be written

\[ X_0 \xrightarrow{F_1} X_1 \xrightarrow{F_2} \cdots \xrightarrow{F_n} X_n. \]

The path is lawful only when every transition is typed and admissible.

32. Path Composite

A lawful path induces

\[ F_{\mathrm{path}} = F_n \circ \cdots \circ F_1. \]

Its actual domain is restricted by every intermediate admissibility condition.

33. Parallel Paths

Suppose two lawful paths connect the same source and target:

\[ F: X \longrightarrow Y \]

and

\[ G: X \longrightarrow Y. \]

BM may ask whether

\[ F(x) = G(x) \]

for all admissible \(x\), or whether they agree under another explicitly chosen notion of equivalence.

34. Path Closure

A network is path-consistent relative to a specified equivalence \(\approx\) when relevant parallel paths satisfy

\[ \boxed{ F_{\mathrm{path\,1}}(x) \approx F_{\mathrm{path\,2}}(x). } \]

The equivalence may be literal equality, isomorphism, homotopy, almost-sure equality, bounded error, or another justified relation.

35. Commutativity

Two operations commute when

\[ \boxed{ F\circ G = G\circ F } \]

wherever both sides are defined.

BM does not assume commutativity.

Operation order must be checked.

36. Noncommuting Operations

If

\[ F\circ G \neq G\circ F, \]

then order carries mathematical information.

This is particularly important for:

differentiation · filtering · localization · projection · reconstruction · coarse-graining · intervention · evolution · quotienting

37. Equalizer-Type Compatibility

If two maps

\[ F,G: X \longrightarrow Y \]

must agree, one may consider the equalizer set

\[ \boxed{ E(F,G) = \left\{ x\in X: F(x)=G(x) \right\}. } \]

This identifies the states on which the two paths are compatible.

38. Pullback-Type Compatibility

Suppose

\[ f: X \longrightarrow Z \]

and

\[ g: Y \longrightarrow Z. \]

Their pullback set is

\[ \boxed{ X\times_Z Y = \left\{ (x,y)\in X\times Y: f(x)=g(y) \right\}. } \]

Such constructions are useful when two Branches must agree on shared interface data.

39. Coequalizer-Type Identification

If

\[ f,g: X \longrightarrow Y, \]

a coequalizing quotient identifies enough of \(Y\) so that

\[ q\circ f = q\circ g. \]

BM can use this kind of construction when lawful identification of parallel outputs is required.

40. Structured Quotients

A quotient

\[ X/{\sim} \]

preserves an operation only when the equivalence relation is compatible with that operation.

For

\[ \sigma: X^n \longrightarrow X, \]

one requires

\[ x_i\sim y_i \quad\forall i \] \[ \Longrightarrow \] \[ \sigma(x_1,\ldots,x_n) \sim \sigma(y_1,\ldots,y_n). \]

41. Realization

A formal BM structure may be abstractly specified before a concrete realization is known.

Let

\[ S \]

denote an abstract specification.

A realization map is

\[ \rho: S \longrightarrow X. \]

The realized object must satisfy the native requirements of \(X\).

42. Realization Failure

A formal specification may fail to realize because:

constraints conflict · required limits do not exist · regularity fails · boundary conditions are inconsistent · positivity fails · topology is incompatible · algebraic relations cannot be satisfied

Formal description alone is not proof of existence.

43. Export

Export moves information from one compatible mathematical context into another representation.

\[ E: X \longrightarrow Y. \]

Export may preserve all information or only selected information.

44. Re-entry

Re-entry inserts exported or transformed information into another compatible Branch.

\[ R: Y \longrightarrow Z. \]

A complete export/re-entry path is

\[ X \xrightarrow{E} Y \xrightarrow{R} Z. \]

45. Re-entry Coherence

Suppose direct transformation is

\[ F: X \longrightarrow Z. \]

A strong coherence condition is

\[ \boxed{ R\circ E = F. } \]

Weaker equivalence may be used when justified by the mathematical domain.

46. Re-entry Loss

If export is non-injective,

\[ E(x)=E(x') \quad\text{for some}\quad x\neq x', \]

then deterministic re-entry cannot distinguish those states using \(E(x)\) alone.

Additional Passport information may be required.

47. Local Versus Global Structure

A local BM structure may be lawful without extending globally.

Let local domains be

\[ U_i \subseteq X. \]

Local objects

\[ s_i \in \mathcal S(U_i) \]

may require agreement on overlaps

\[ U_i\cap U_j. \]

48. Gluing Condition

If restriction maps exist, a standard compatibility condition is

\[ s_i \big| _{U_i\cap U_j} = s_j \big| _{U_i\cap U_j}. \]

Under appropriate mathematical assumptions, compatible local pieces may admit a global glued object.

Whether such gluing exists depends on the native mathematics.

49. Coherence

Coherence means that different lawful constructions intended to represent the same mathematical process agree under the relevant equivalence.

For two composites

\[ F_2\circ F_1 \]

and

\[ G_2\circ G_1, \]

one may require

\[ F_2\circ F_1 \approx G_2\circ G_1. \]

50. Higher Coherence

Large BM networks may contain many overlapping compatible paths.

Pairwise agreement may not be enough to guarantee global consistency.

Higher coherence studies compatibility among larger families of transformations, interfaces, and compositions.

Status: active formal development.

51. Deterministic Structure

A deterministic structural transformation uses a function

\[ F: X \longrightarrow Y. \]

Each admissible input has one output.

52. Stochastic Structure

A stochastic Branch or operation may use

\[ M: X \longrightarrow \mathcal P(Y). \]

Its output is a probability law.

Stochastic composition must preserve probability semantics.

53. Nondeterministic Structure

A nondeterministic relation may be

\[ R \subseteq X\times Y. \]

Its output set is

\[ R(x) = \{y\in Y:xRy\}. \]

Existential and universal requirements must remain distinct.

54. Finite-Arity Interaction

An ordinary \(n\)-ary BM interaction may use

\[ I: X_1\times\cdots\times X_n \longrightarrow Y. \]

This sector is mathematically straightforward when the required product exists and the domain is properly specified.

55. Indexed or Infinite Interaction

For an indexed family

\[ \{X_i\}_{i\in I}, \]

an interaction may formally involve

\[ \prod_{i\in I}X_i. \]

Infinite products, limits, convergence, topology, measurability, and other domain-specific issues must be checked rather than assumed.

56. Structure Preservation

A map

\[ F: X \longrightarrow Y \]

is structure-preserving only relative to a specified structure.

For a binary operation \(\star\), one might require

\[ \boxed{ F(x\star y) = F(x)\star' F(y). } \]

Other structures require different preservation laws.

57. Invariant Preservation

Let

\[ C_X: X \longrightarrow V \]

and

\[ C_Y: Y \longrightarrow V. \]

Transformation \(F:X\to Y\) preserves the invariant when

\[ \boxed{ C_Y(F(x)) = C_X(x). } \]

58. Structural Failure Modes

Failure Meaning
Type failure output is not accepted by the receiving structure
Domain failure input lies outside the admissible operation domain
Passport failure required distinctions or conditions are unavailable
Law failure native equations or identities are violated
Path incoherence parallel paths disagree when they are required to agree
Quotient failure equivalence is not compatible with required operations
Realization failure formal specification has no admissible realization
Re-entry failure exported object cannot be lawfully interpreted by receiver
Gluing failure local structures cannot be consistently combined

59. Worked Example: Typed Composition

Let

\[ F: \mathbb R \longrightarrow \mathbb R^2 \]

be

\[ F(x) = (x,x^2), \]

and let

\[ G: \mathbb R^2 \longrightarrow \mathbb R \]

be

\[ G(u,v) = u+v. \]

Then

\[ (G\circ F)(x) = x+x^2. \]

The types match because \(F\) outputs an element of \(\mathbb R^2\), which is the domain of \(G\).

60. Worked Example: Domain Restriction

Let

\[ G(u,v) = \sqrt{v}. \]

over real numbers.

Its admissible domain is

\[ D_G = \left\{ (u,v)\in\mathbb R^2: v\ge0 \right\}. \]

If

\[ F(x) = (x,x^2), \]

then

\[ F(x) \in D_G \qquad \forall x\in\mathbb R. \]

Hence the composite is defined for all real \(x\).

61. Worked Example: Noncommuting Operations

Let

\[ F(x)=x+1 \]

and

\[ G(x)=2x. \]

Then

\[ (F\circ G)(x) = 2x+1, \]

while

\[ (G\circ F)(x) = 2x+2. \]

Therefore

\[ F\circ G \neq G\circ F. \]

Operation order is mathematically significant.

62. Quantitative Structure Diagnostics

Some structural questions can be assigned measurable quantities.

For approximate path coherence one may define

\[ \Delta(x) = d \left( F(x), G(x) \right). \]

Exact coherence gives

\[ \Delta(x)=0. \]

Approximate coherence may require

\[ \Delta(x) \le \varepsilon. \]

Quantities like \(\Delta(x)\) can later support genuine MATLAB-style plots across state or parameter space.

63. Scientific Visualization Opportunities

Structural mathematics can produce quantitative figures when there is a meaningful numerical object to plot.

path error versus state · compatibility defect versus parameter · invariant drift · convergence of iterated operations · network response magnitude · condition numbers · admissibility regions · stability regions

These are equation-driven figures.

They are different from BM structural diagrams, which remain unrecreated.

65. Structural Status

Core accepted roles

Branch, Interaction, Leaf, Tree, and Response are treated as core BM structural roles.

Active extended machinery

Passports, Roots, Operations, Configurations, interfaces, realization, export, re-entry, quotienting, and coherence are part of the active integrated BM architecture.

Candidate or developing structures

Some forms of Rooting, Root Passport transfer, higher coherence, Seed machinery, and other extensions remain under active formal development.

66. What the Structural Vocabulary Does Not Mean

Calling something a Branch does not change its native mathematics.

Calling something an Interaction does not make an invalid operation lawful.

Calling an output a Root does not guarantee future compatibility.

Calling information a Passport does not prove sufficiency.

Calling several operations a Tree does not establish coherence.

67. Structural Diagram Status

Formal BM diagrams have not yet been recreated for this public encyclopedia.

No substitute diagrams are being invented.

The final public diagrams will be added only after the underlying diagram registry and canonical relationships have been reconstructed and checked.

68. Central Structural Principle

The structural architecture of BM can be summarized as:

\[ \boxed{ \text{Every mathematical object keeps its type, every operation keeps its law, and every interface must justify the passage between them.} } \]

The purpose of the structural registry is to make those requirements explicit rather than leaving them implicit inside a large multi-structure calculation.
